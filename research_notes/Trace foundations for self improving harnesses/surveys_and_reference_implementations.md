## 1. What do the major surveys say (what evolves, when, how, required infrastructure, open problems, evaluation practice)?

### Takeaway
The two general surveys agree on the object model: an agent system is a model plus context (prompt and memory), tools and an architecture or workflow, and any of the four can be the thing that changes. They differ in emphasis: Gao et al. organise by what / when / how / where and give the fullest evaluation and guardrail material; Fang et al. organise around one optimisation loop and three ordered laws. Neither states a build order, and neither lists "infrastructure" as a section; requirements have to be read out of their evaluation protocols and safety checklists.

### Cited Findings

**Gao et al., "A Survey of Self-Evolving Agents" (arXiv 2507.21046; v4 dated 16 January 2026)**
- Formal object: an agent system Π = (Γ, {ψᵢ}, {Cᵢ}, {𝒲ᵢ}), where Γ is the control-flow or collaboration topology and each node has a model ψ, a context C (prompt P and memory M) and a tool set 𝒲. A self-evolving strategy is a function f(Π, τ, r) = Π′ that maps the current system, a trajectory τ and feedback r to a new system. — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- Three inclusion criteria for "self-evolving": updates depend on experience; updates persist and change the policy; the system explores or initiates learning itself. — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- **What to evolve**: models (policy, experience-based); context, split into memory evolution (examples named: Mem0, ReasoningBank, Agent Workflow Memory) and prompt optimisation (APE, ProTeGi, PromptBreeder, ACE, DSPy, TextGrad); tools (creation, mastery by refinement, management and selection); architecture, single-agent (Darwin Gödel Machine, AlphaEvolve) and multi-agent (ADAS, AFlow, MaAS, ScoreFlow). — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- **When to evolve**: intra-test-time (during a task) and inter-test-time (between tasks, offline or online), each realisable by in-context learning, supervised fine-tuning or reinforcement learning. — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- **How to evolve**: reward-based (textual feedback, internal, external and implicit rewards); imitation and demonstration (self-generated, cross-agent, hybrid); population-based and evolutionary. Cross-cutting dimensions: online versus offline, on-policy versus off-policy, reward granularity. — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- **Where**: general domain versus specialised domains (coding, GUI, finance, medicine, education). — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- **Evaluation goals and metrics** (their Table 6, paraphrased by the fetch tool): Adaptivity (success rate by iteration step, adaptation speed); Retention (forgetting FGT, backward transfer BWT); Generalisation (aggregate multi-domain performance, out-of-domain performance); Efficiency (tokens, time, steps, tool productivity, and cost-per-gain = total cost at horizon t divided by the performance gain at t); Safety (safety score, harm score, completion under policy, risk ratio, refusal rate, leakage rate). — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- **Three evaluation paradigms**: static assessment (SWE-bench series, GAIA, WebArena, OSWorld, AgentBench, plus component tests for planning, tool use and memory); short-horizon adaptive assessment (existing benchmarks tracked over iterations, as ADAS on ARC and AWM on WebArena; MemoryAgentBench's test-time-learning dimension); long-horizon lifelong assessment (LTMBenchmark, LifelongAgentBench, AutoEnv), which the authors call "nascent but critical". — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- **Protocol requirements**: short-horizon protocols assume no state persists across tasks and set a per-task budget; long-horizon protocols assume "full persistence of model parameters, prompts, memories, and toolsets across tasks" with stage and cumulative budgets. — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- **Stated limitation**: "true apples-to-apples comparison remains infeasible at present", because of inconsistent reporting of latency, cost and safety and non-comparable pipelines and backbones. — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- **Challenges**: personalisation; generalisation (scalable architecture, cross-domain adaptation, continual learning and catastrophic forgetting, knowledge transferability); safety and controllability; multi-agent ecosystems. — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- Companion list: CharlesQ9/Self-Evolving-Agents, 1,350 stars, last push 2025-10-15 (over a year without a push). — [GitHub](https://github.com/CharlesQ9/Self-Evolving-Agents)

**Fang et al., "A Comprehensive Survey of Self-Evolving AI Agents" (arXiv 2508.07407; v1 10 August 2025, v2 31 August 2025; no venue listed)**
- Organises methods by which component the optimiser acts on: single-agent optimisation (LLM behaviour, prompt, memory, tool), multi-agent optimisation (prompts, topology, unified, LLM backbone) and domain-specific optimisation (biomedicine, programming, finance and law). — [arXiv 2508.07407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07407v2)
- Prompt optimisation is subdivided into edit-based, generative, text-gradient-based and evolutionary; memory into short-term and long-term; tool optimisation into training-based, inference-time and tool-functionality optimisation. — [arXiv 2508.07407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07407v2)
- Frames a four-stage paradigm shift: Model Offline Pretraining (MOP), Model Online Adaptation (MOA), Multi-Agent Orchestration (MAO), Multi-Agent Self-Evolving (MASE). — [arXiv 2508.07407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07407v2)
- Evaluation section covers benchmark-based evaluation by agent type, LLM-as-a-judge, agent-as-a-judge (judging "the entire reasoning trajectory", not only final outputs), and safety evaluation. It states: "Evaluation is no longer viewed as a static endpoint but as a dynamic feedback mechanism". — [arXiv 2508.07407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07407v2)
- Challenges are grouped under its three laws. Endure: safety, regulation and alignment; reward-model and optimisation instability. Excel: evaluation where ground truth is absent or disputed; efficiency versus effectiveness; "Optimised prompts or agent topologies are often brittle, showing poor generalisation across LLM backbones". Evolve: multimodal and spatial environments; tool creation (most methods "assume a fixed toolset"). — [arXiv 2508.07407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07407v2)
- Future directions include simulated environments for closed-loop optimisation and "benchmarks and protocols that reflect real-world complexity, support interaction-based and longitudinal assessment". — [arXiv 2508.07407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07407v2)
- Companion list: Awesome-Self-Evolving-Agents, 2,527 stars, last push 2026-05-16. — [GitHub](https://github.com/EvoAgentX/Awesome-Self-Evolving-Agents)

**Narrower surveys (abstract level only)**
- "Memory in the Age of AI Agents" (Hu et al., 47 authors; v1 15 December 2025, v2 13 January 2026): classifies memory by form (token-level, parametric, latent), function (factual, experiential, working) and dynamics (formation, evolution, retrieval); open frontiers are memory automation, reinforcement-learning integration, multimodal and multi-agent memory, and trustworthiness. — [arXiv 2512.13564](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.13564)
- "From Static Templates to Dynamic Runtime Graphs: A Survey of Workflow Optimization for LLM Agents" (Yue et al., IBM and others; v1 23 March 2026): treats workflows as "agentic computation graphs"; classifies by when structure is fixed (static scaffold before deployment versus per-run selection or revision), what is optimised, and the feedback signal (task metrics, verifier signals, preferences, trace-derived feedback). It separates three artefacts: reusable templates, run-specific realised graphs, and execution traces. It proposes adding graph-level properties, execution cost and robustness to task metrics. — [arXiv 2603.22386](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22386)
- "From Question Answering to Task Completion: A Survey on Agent System and Harness Design" (Guo et al., 17 authors; v1 14 June 2026): decomposes the harness into six runtime responsibilities (observation, context, control, action, state, verification) and traces engineering paradigms from prompt engineering through workflows, context engineering and harness engineering to agent-native training with co-evolution. Open problems: value-aware evaluation, safety, harness generalisation, model-harness co-evolution. — [arXiv 2606.20683](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.20683)
- The coding-specific survey (Zhou et al., arXiv 2608.03392) is covered in the round-one notes and was not re-read. — [arXiv 2608.03392](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03392)

**Overview post and workshop framing**
- Lilian Weng, "Harness Engineering for Self-Improvement" (4 July 2026; read through a summarising fetch tool): defines a harness as "the system surrounding a base model that orchestrates execution", and describes the optimisation target as progressing "instruction prompts → structured context → workflow → harness code → optimizer code". The Meta-Harness repository's onboarding file points readers to this post as its recommended broader overview. — [Lil'Log](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2026-07-04-harness/); [Meta-Harness ONBOARDING.md](https://github.com/stanford-iris-lab/meta-harness/blob/main/ONBOARDING.md)
- The ICLR 2026 Workshop on AI with Recursive Self-Improvement (26 April 2026, Rio de Janeiro; organisers include Mingchen Zhuge and Jürgen Schmidhuber; invited speakers include Chelsea Finn, Jeff Clune and Sergey Levine) frames contributions through six lenses: what changes, when, how, where systems operate, "alignment, security, and safety" (long-horizon stability and regression risk), and evaluation and benchmarks. Its call also names instrumentation and rollback. — [RSI workshop site](https://recursive-workshop.github.io/)

### Inferences
- The what / when / how axes are shared by Gao et al., the RSI workshop call and (in different words) Fang et al., so they can be treated as the field's common vocabulary. For a coding-agent harness with a frozen model, the reachable cells are: what = context (prompt, memory), tools and skills, workflow or harness code; when = between tasks; how = textual feedback plus population or archive search.
- The 2025 surveys mostly predate the 2026 "harness" framing. The harness survey (Guo et al.) and Weng's post add the fifth target the earlier taxonomies lack a clean slot for: the harness code itself, and beyond it the optimiser's own code.
- No survey prescribes a build order. The closest thing to a required-infrastructure list is implied by Gao et al.'s long-horizon protocol (persistent state, budgets, per-stage measurement) and by their deployment checklist in section 5.

### Gaps
- The Gao et al. venue: round-one notes record TMLR 2026; the arXiv HTML page read here shows an OpenReview link but names no venue. Not resolved.
- Gao et al. tables were paraphrased by the fetch tool; exact wording and the final 98,000 characters of the page (references and appendices) were not read.
- Not read: a survey of context engineering (for example arXiv 2507.13334), any standalone 2026 harness-engineering survey other than Guo et al., and the full text of the memory, workflow and harness surveys. (2605.29682) is a different paper ("Scaling Laws for Agent Harnesses via Effective Feedback Compute"), so those surveys are unverified and uncited.

## 2. Is there a commonly drawn reference loop, where do logging, evaluation and safety sit in it, and what comes first?

### Takeaway
Yes. Fang et al. draw it explicitly as four boxes (system inputs, agent system, environment, optimiser), and every open-source implementation inspected is the same loop with one addition the survey figure omits: a persistent archive of candidates with scores and parent links. In the survey's figure, evaluation lives inside the environment box, logging is not drawn at all, and safety is a constraint stated in prose; the code is where logging and the archive appear.

### Cited Findings
- Fang et al., Figure 3 caption (verbatim): "The process forms an iterative optimisation loop comprising four components: System Inputs, Agent System, Environment, and Optimiser. System inputs define the task setting (e.g., task-level or instance-level). The agent system (in single- or multi-agent form) executes the specified task. The environment (depending on different scenarios) provides feedback via proxy metrics. The optimiser updates the agent system through a defined search space and optimisation algorithm until performance goals are met." — [arXiv 2508.07407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07407v2)
- System inputs for the common task-level setting are a task description plus a training dataset, I = {T, D_train}, with a separate test set for evaluating the optimised agent; where labelled data is missing, some methods synthesise a surrogate dataset. — [arXiv 2508.07407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07407v2)
- The environment "generates feedback signals, which are derived from predefined evaluation metrics"; for code generation it "may include code execution and verification components such as compilers, interpreters, and test cases"; where ground truth is unavailable, "LLM-based evaluators are often employed". — [arXiv 2508.07407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07407v2)
- The optimiser is defined by two parts: a search space S ("ranging from agent prompts or tool selection strategies to continuous LLM parameters or architectural structures") and an optimisation algorithm H ("rule-based heuristics, gradient descent, Bayesian optimisation, Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), reinforcement learning, evolutionary strategies, or learning-based policies"). The objective is A* = argmax over S of O(A; I), a scalar evaluation function. — [arXiv 2508.07407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07407v2)
- The loop "terminates once a predefined performance threshold is reached or convergence criteria are satisfied", and the survey names EvoAgentX as built on this framework. — [arXiv 2508.07407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07407v2)
- Gao et al. express the same loop as an update rule, Πⱼ₊₁ = f(Πⱼ, τⱼ, rⱼ): the inputs to an update are the trajectory and the feedback. — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- ShinkaEvolve's documentation states the loop as six steps: sample a parent or inspiration context from the database and archive; ask LLMs to propose a patch or full program; validate and materialise the candidate; run the evaluator and collect metrics; persist the result into the program database; update archive, island and optional meta-memory state. — [ShinkaEvolve docs/core_concepts.md](https://github.com/SakanaAI/ShinkaEvolve/blob/main/docs/core_concepts.md)
- SICA's README states the loop as four steps: evaluate the current agent on benchmark tasks; store the results in an archive; run the agent on its own codebase to work on an improvement; repeat with the updated code. — [SICA README](https://github.com/MaximeRobeyns/self_improving_coding_agent)
- Weng's overview describes the recurring cycle as proposer, evaluator, archive and selection, and says permission and security layers "need to live outside this loop". — [Lil'Log](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2026-07-04-harness/)
- On what must exist before search starts, Meta-Harness's onboarding file gives a fit checklist: "There are repeated tasks on which to evaluate candidate harnesses"; "Success can be evaluated consistently, including through preferences when appropriate"; "Harness changes could improve performance while the base model stays fixed". It adds: "If an item is unclear, resolve it before planning the search." — [Meta-Harness ONBOARDING.md](https://github.com/stanford-iris-lab/meta-harness/blob/main/ONBOARDING.md)
- The same file's required contents for a domain specification, in its order: the task and success criterion; what the harness may change and what stays fixed; how candidates are evaluated and selected, including separation from final testing; the starting baseline, search budget and stopping rule; "The execution evidence saved for diagnosing failures"; "The smallest first implementation and its acceptance check". — [Meta-Harness ONBOARDING.md](https://github.com/stanford-iris-lab/meta-harness/blob/main/ONBOARDING.md)
- On evidence, the same file says: "Save candidate source, evaluation configuration, scores, and execution traces, including errors and costs when available. Associate every result with the exact candidate that produced it. Start with ordinary files and directories." — [Meta-Harness ONBOARDING.md](https://github.com/stanford-iris-lab/meta-harness/blob/main/ONBOARDING.md)

### Inferences
- The consensus reference loop, merging the survey figure with the code, has six parts: (1) task inputs with a train or search split and a held-out split; (2) the editable agent system with a declared editable surface; (3) an environment that executes and scores; (4) a trace and result store keyed to the exact candidate; (5) an archive with lineage; (6) an optimiser made of a proposer and a selection rule. Parts 4 and 5 are absent from Fang et al.'s figure and present in every repository in section 3.
- Logging sits between environment and optimiser: it is the optimiser's input. Evaluation sits in the environment and, in every well-evidenced system, outside the editable surface. Safety is not a box in any drawn loop; in code it appears as (a) isolation around execution, (b) a fixed evaluator and archive, and (c) an acceptance gate between proposal and archive.
- "What comes first" is not stated by the surveys. The order supported by the sources is: a repeatable task set and a consistent success measure; a runnable baseline measured under the same conditions; saved per-candidate evidence (source, config, scores, traces) in plain files; only then a proposer and a search rule. Meta-Harness's onboarding file is the only source read that writes this down as a sequence, and it is guidance from one research group, not a survey finding.

### Gaps
- Gao et al. may contain a loop figure; the fetched text did not expose figures, so only their update rule is reported.
- I did not open the EvoAgentX paper (Wang et al., 2025) to check how it maps its modules to Fang et al.'s four boxes; section 3 reports its repository layout instead.

## 3. What do the open-source implementations actually contain?

### Takeaway
The research artefacts (DGM, SICA, ADAS, AFlow) are small scripts that have not been pushed to for 11 to 22 months, while the maintained code is the general optimisers (OpenEvolve, ShinkaEvolve, GEPA, DSPy). Across all of them the on-disk record converges on the same five things: the candidate itself, a parent pointer, per-task scores, the raw proposer and evaluator output, and a run-level index. Evaluator isolation is much weaker in code than the papers' safety sections suggest: several run generated code in the optimiser's own process.

**Darwin Gödel Machine** (1,650 files, of which 1,593 are the initial agent's stored benchmark logs)
- Layout: `DGM_outer.py` (outer loop), `self_improve_step.py` (one self-modification plus evaluation), `coding_agent.py`, `tools/` (`bash.py`, `edit.py` only), `prompts/` (self-improvement and diagnosis prompts), `swe_bench/` and `polyglot/` evaluation harnesses with fixed task subsets (`subsets/small.json`, `medium.json`, `big.json`), `utils/` (docker, git, evaluation), `analysis/` (archive visualisation). — [DGM tree](https://github.com/jennyzzt/dgm)
- Written per run, under `output_dgm/` by default: an append-only `dgm_metadata.jsonl` with one row per generation including the full archive list; per candidate a directory holding `metadata.json`, `self_improve.log`, `self_evo.md` (the proposer's chat history, copied out of the container) and `model_patch.diff`. — [DGM_outer.py](https://github.com/jennyzzt/dgm/blob/main/DGM_outer.py); [self_improve_step.py](https://github.com/jennyzzt/dgm/blob/main/self_improve_step.py)
- Lineage metadata fields in a candidate's `metadata.json`: `run_id`, `parent_commit`, `entry` (the task chosen to motivate the change), `problem_statement`, `model_patch_exists`, `model_patch_notempty`, `is_compiled`, `improvement_diagnosis`, and `overall_performance` with `accuracy_score`, `total_resolved_ids`, `total_unresolved_ids`, `total_emptypatch_ids`, `total_submitted_instances`. The shipped `initial/metadata.json` records 12 resolved of 60 submitted (0.2). — [self_improve_step.py](https://github.com/jennyzzt/dgm/blob/main/self_improve_step.py); [initial/metadata.json](https://github.com/jennyzzt/dgm/blob/main/initial/metadata.json)
- The candidate is stored as a diff against its parent, not as a full copy; the child container receives the parent's patch as `/dgm/parent_patch.txt`. — [self_improve_step.py](https://github.com/jennyzzt/dgm/blob/main/self_improve_step.py)
- Isolation: each self-improvement step runs in a Docker container with a 30-minute `timeout` on the agent command and a 1.5-hour timeout on the worker future. The container is started with `client.containers.run(image=image_name, name=container_name, detach=True)`; no network, memory or capability restrictions appear in `utils/docker_utils.py`. — [utils/docker_utils.py](https://github.com/jennyzzt/dgm/blob/main/utils/docker_utils.py)
- Knobs (`DGM_outer.py`): `--max_generation` 80, `--selfimprove_size` 2, `--selfimprove_workers` 2, `--update_archive` (`keep_all` default, or `keep_better`), `--num_swe_evals` 1, `--eval_noise` 0.1, `--shallow_eval`, `--no_full_eval`, `--post_improve_diagnose`, `--continue_from`, `--run_baseline` (`no_selfimprove`, `no_darwin`), `--polyglot`. — [DGM_outer.py](https://github.com/jennyzzt/dgm/blob/main/DGM_outer.py)
- The README warns that the repository "involves executing untrusted, model-generated code" and links a Google Drive folder with all foundation-model output logs from the paper's experiments. — [DGM README](https://github.com/jennyzzt/dgm)

During benchmark runs the experiment directory is mounted read-write. Per-problem timeout is 10 minutes with a 10-second grace period. The `docker run` arguments read set no network or resource limits. — [runner.py](https://github.com/MaximeRobeyns/self_improving_coding_agent/blob/master/runner.py)
- The README's own to-do list includes "reduce the variance of self-improvement runs (early features often influence subsequent features)". — [SICA README](https://github.com/MaximeRobeyns/self_improving_coding_agent)

### Inferences
- A de facto per-candidate record exists across DGM, SICA, OpenEvolve, ShinkaEvolve, GEPA, ADAS, AFlow and Meta-Harness. Its common fields are: candidate id; parent id (or parent list); the candidate's content or a diff against the parent; per-task scores as well as the aggregate; generation or iteration number and timestamp; the proposer's raw output or rationale; evaluator output or error text. Fields present in only the more mature tools: cost accounting (ShinkaEvolve), a failed-attempt log separate from the candidate table (ShinkaEvolve `attempt_log`, OpenEvolve `include_failures`), public versus private metrics (ShinkaEvolve), and per-item usage counters (ACE `bullet_usage_log.jsonl`, Hermes `.usage.json`).
- Two storage styles are in use: plain files and directories (DGM, SICA, ADAS, AFlow, ACE, Meta-Harness, GEPA) and a database (ShinkaEvolve's SQLite, OpenEvolve's in-memory database with checkpoint dumps). The harness-search systems whose proposer is a coding agent all use plain files, which fits Meta-Harness's instruction to "Start with ordinary files and directories" and its finding (round one) that the proposer benefits from reading raw files.
- Failed and rejected candidates are under-recorded by default. OpenEvolve leaves its trace off and excludes failures unless asked; DGM keeps a failed candidate's directory but not in the archive list. A harness layer that wants to learn from failed proposals has to choose to keep them.
- Isolation in code falls into four grades: none (ADAS `exec()`; OpenEvolve in-process import; ShinkaEvolve local default; GEPA core), container without network or resource limits (DGM, SICA), OS-level jail around the proposer with the network left open (GEPA `optimize_anything`), and a trusted controller with leakage rules and an evaluation budget (Meta-Harness Harbor pilot, marked experimental). None of the repositories inspected implements the "no default host, network, or process access" item from Gao et al.'s checklist.
- The paper-artefact repositories are frozen snapshots: DGM (last push August 2025), SICA (April 2025), ADAS (January 2025), AFlow (December 2025). They are reference designs to read, not dependencies to build on. The actively maintained candidates for reuse are OpenEvolve, ShinkaEvolve, GEPA and DSPy.

### Gaps
- Not opened beyond repository metadata: HGM, HyperAgents, `letta-ai/letta-code` source, the Meta-Harness Terminal-Bench 2 artefact repository, the `terminal_bench_2` reference experiment's scripts, and DGM's Google Drive log folder.
- DSPy was inspected only at the level of the optimiser file list and the GEPA wrapper's signature; what MIPROv2 or SIMBA write to disk was not read.
- OpenEvolve's `process_parallel.py` was not read, so whether evaluations run in worker processes (which would give crash isolation but not sandboxing) is unconfirmed. The name of the directory holding `checkpoint_<N>/` was not captured.
- SICA and DGM container isolation statements rest on the specific lines read; a Dockerfile-level restriction (for example a non-root user) was not checked.
- The kayba sandbox's actual restrictions were not read past the module header.
- AFlow's `results.json` and `processed_experience.json` returned empty when fetched for GSM8K, so their schemas are described from the optimiser code that reads and writes them, not from a sample.
- Stars measure attention, not use; no download or dependent-repository counts were collected.

## 4. Which frameworks are positioned as general toolkits, and what must the user supply?

### Takeaway
Four are positioned as general: GEPA (with DSPy as its most common front end), OpenEvolve and ShinkaEvolve (for anything expressible as a program plus an evaluator), EvoAgentX (for agent workflows) and Microsoft Trace (as a library primitive). All of them require the same three things from the user: a starting artefact, a repeatable evaluation that returns a number, and a budget; the better ones also want diagnostic text alongside the number. Only GEPA and ShinkaEvolve define a place for trace or feedback text in their contract, and none defines a portable trace format.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- The universal contract is `evaluate(candidate, examples) -> per-example scores (+ optional per-example diagnostics)`. Per-example granularity matters: GEPA's Pareto selection, DGM's resolved and unresolved id lists and Meta-Harness's per-dataset frontier all depend on it. A harness layer that records only an aggregate pass rate cannot be plugged into any of these.
- None of the toolkits will supply the evaluation set. For a coding-agent harness in an ordinary repository this is the missing input, which matches the round-one finding that metric-driven optimisers "need an evaluation set that ordinary repositories do not have". Building or mining that task set is therefore the precondition for using any of them.
- The trace is treated as opaque by the frameworks (GEPA says so explicitly). The format is the user's choice; the only fixed requirement is that traces align one-to-one with examples and scores, and that the user can render them into text for a reflecting model. A harness layer is free to define its own trace schema, provided each trace is keyed to a task instance and a candidate.
- The toolkits are now designed to be driven by coding agents (skills in GEPA and ShinkaEvolve; CLI-agent backends in OpenEvolve and GEPA). That makes "the harness calls an optimiser" and "the optimiser calls the harness's agent as proposer" both realistic integration shapes.

### Gaps
- EvoAgentX's `Benchmark` and `Evaluator` interfaces were not read in detail, so the exact methods a user must implement are not listed.
- Trace's newer trainer and guide abstractions, if any exist on other branches or in a successor package, were not inspected.
- LangMem, TextGrad's own repository, AdalFlow and Opik's optimiser were not examined.
- The "50+ production uses" claim and the third-party projects listed by Meta-Harness were not verified.

## 5. What safety and governance requirements do the surveys and frameworks state, and how concretely are they implemented?

### Takeaway
The surveys state requirements at two levels: ordered principles (Fang et al.'s three laws, safety before performance before autonomy) and a concrete deployment checklist (Gao et al.'s Table 12: sandboxing, an immutable audit log, versioning with tagged safe states, tested rollback, monitoring, approval gates). The code implements the lineage and audit items almost everywhere, the held-out-data item in the better systems, and the sandbox, rollback and approval items rarely; the only tested rollback found is in Hermes's curator, which has no evaluator.

### Cited Findings

**Principles**
- Fang et al., verbatim: "I. Endure (Safety Adaptation): Self-evolving AI agents must maintain safety and stability during any modification; II. Excel (Performance Preservation): Subject to the First law, self-evolving AI agents must preserve or enhance existing task performance; III. Evolve (Autonomous Evolution): Subject to the First and Second law, self-evolving AI agents must be able to autonomously optimise their internal components in response to changing tasks, environments, or resources." The authors say the laws are inspired by Asimov's Three Laws of Robotics. — [arXiv 2508.07407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07407v2)
- Fang et al. on what Endure implies for evaluation: "safety is not a one-off certification but an ongoing requirement: every evolution step, from prompt updates to topology changes, must be assessed for unintended or malicious behaviours." They add that "most current evaluations are snapshot-based" and that evolution-aware safety benchmarks remain "an open and urgent challenge". — [arXiv 2508.07407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07407v2)
- Fang et al. on governance: evolving agents undermine "existing legal frameworks (e.g., EU AI Act, GDPR), which assume static models and fixed decision logic", calling for "evolution-aware audit mechanisms, adaptive licences, provable-safety sandboxes". — [arXiv 2508.07407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07407v2)

**Checklist (Gao et al., Table 12, paraphrased by the fetch tool)**
- Tool and code safety: run all tools and generated code in an isolated sandbox with no default host, network or process access; enforce CPU, memory and time limits; run automated security checks on new or modified tools; scan third-party dependencies; classify tools by risk and require approval for high-risk ones. — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- Self-modification control: keep an immutable audit log of all self-modifications; version the agent's state and tag known-safe versions; maintain a tested one-step rollback; check updated models against a safety-critical test set before deployment. — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- Behavioural safety: monitor runtime actions; watch key metrics for reward hacking and alert on sudden unexplained changes; continuous red-teaming; human review for any change to core goals or safety constraints. — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- Memory integrity and privacy: defences to detect and isolate poisoned memories; redact personal data before storage; retention limits. — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- Operational governance: human approval for high-stakes actions; an incident-response plan with shutdown and rollback; an oversight dashboard; an audit trail that links each action to the memories, goals or inferences behind it. — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- The same survey names three emergent risk classes: behaviour drift in model evolution, "Deployment-time reward hacking in memory evolution", and unsafe self-created or ingested tools. — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)

**What the code implements**

- The RSI workshop call asks authors to add "a brief note on risks, limitations, and mitigations" when work touches self-improving behaviour or tool access, and names rollback and instrumentation as in-scope topics. — [RSI workshop site](https://recursive-workshop.github.io/)

### Inferences
- The three laws translate directly into an acceptance order for a harness change: first, it must not weaken a guard, gate or sensor (Endure); second, it must not regress existing task performance (Excel); only then is "it improves something" (Evolve) considered. No inspected repository encodes this ordering; they gate on the third alone or on the second and third together.
- Lineage is the one requirement the field has standardised in code. Everything else in Gao et al.'s checklist is at best partially implemented, and the gap is widest for the two items a deployed harness most needs: tested rollback of an applied change, and a regression gate with a defined bar.
- The distinction between an optimiser run and a deployed system explains the gap. The optimiser repositories produce a best candidate and stop; "which version is live, and how do I revert it" is outside their scope. Hermes's curator is the only inspected code that manages a live library, and it solves rollback with snapshots but has no measurement. A self-improvement layer for a working harness needs both halves, and no single open-source project inspected provides both.
- Default settings lean permissive: OpenEvolve's edit-region enforcement and trace are off, ShinkaEvolve runs locally, Hermes's consolidation is off but its deterministic pruning is on. Safe behaviour has to be chosen, not inherited.

### Gaps
- No repository was found that implements a tamper check on the evaluator itself (for example hashing evaluation code before and after a proposer run). The Meta-Harness Harbor pilot comes closest by never importing candidate code into the controller.
- Hyperagents and HGM source was not inspected, so whether they add safeguards beyond DGM's is unknown.
- AHE, Self-Harness, GSME and RRSI repositories (the 2026 systems whose papers describe regression and significance gates) were not inspected; the Meta-Harness README links an RRSI code repository at `google-research/rrsi` that was not opened.
- Gao et al.'s checklist is reported from a paraphrase; the exact wording should be checked before quoting.

## 6. What is unsolved, and what benchmarks measure self-improvement or continual learning of agents?

### Takeaway
The surveys agree that long-horizon evaluation, forgetting, transfer across model backbones, cost, and safety under continued modification are open, and that results across methods are not yet comparable. In 2026 a group of stream-style benchmarks appeared that test these directly; their early results are sobering: gains on a task stream are a few points, come with measurable interference and forgetting, and are not reliably better than plain in-context learning.

### Cited Findings

**What the surveys list as unsolved**
- Long-horizon evaluation: Gao et al. call lifelong assessment "nascent but critical" and say short-horizon tests make it "challenging to assess long-term knowledge retention"; Fang et al. call for "longitudinal assessment" aligned "with long-term improvement signals". — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046); [arXiv 2508.07407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07407v2)
- Catastrophic forgetting: Gao et al. describe the stability-plasticity dilemma as particularly acute for foundation-model agents and say balancing efficiency against drift "remains a significant open challenge". — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- Transfer: Fang et al. say optimised prompts and topologies show "poor generalisation across LLM backbones with differing reasoning abilities", which "undermines scalability and reusability in production settings". — [arXiv 2508.07407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07407v2)
- Cost: Fang et al. say designing methods "that explicitly trade off effectiveness against efficiency remains unresolved"; Gao et al. propose cost-per-gain and note cost grows non-linearly with the complexity of adaptation mechanisms. — [arXiv 2508.07407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07407v2); [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- Feedback quality: Fang et al. note that in several domains "reliable ground truth is often absent or disputed", and that learned reward models suffer "noisy supervision, and feedback inconsistency". — [arXiv 2508.07407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07407v2)
- Comparability: Gao et al.: "true apples-to-apples comparison remains infeasible at present". Their own worked protocol example reports a success rate rising from 21.80% to 30.29% but no forgetting, backward-transfer or cost-per-gain figures. — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)
- Weng's list of open problems (via summarising fetch): weak or fuzzy evaluators; context and memory lifecycle; under-reported negative results; diversity collapse; reward hacking; long-term success such as maintainability that short sandbox rewards miss; and the human role ("Humans should move up the stack, not be removed from the loop"). — [Lil'Log](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2026-07-04-harness/)
- The harness survey lists harness generalisation and model-harness co-evolution as open. — [arXiv 2606.20683](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.20683)

**Benchmarks named in the 2025 surveys**
- LifelongAgentBench (interdependent task sequences across database, operating system and knowledge-graph environments), LTMBenchmark (long-term memory through dynamic conversational tests, reporting accuracy, a memory-span-weighted score, tests per hour and cost), AutoEnv / AUTOENV-36 (generated heterogeneous environments for cross-environment adaptation), MemoryAgentBench (test-time-learning dimension), and StuLife (a "campus-life" benchmark). — [arXiv 2507.21046](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21046)

**2026 stream benchmarks and studies**
- **"Are Self-Evolving Agents Continual Learners?"** (Chen et al., Mila and Google DeepMind; arXiv 2610.25858v1, 19 October 2026). Evaluates ReasoningBank, GEPA and Meta-Harness, with weights frozen (Gemini 3.6 Flash in a ReAct loop), on streams built from BIRD, BFCL and ScienceWorld with 50 training tasks per group under topic, workflow, domain and compound drift. Overall averages in points over the seed harness (terminal / average / plasticity / stability): ReasoningBank +2.5 / +2.0 / +3.3 / −1.9; GEPA +3.7 / +3.1 / +6.2 / −4.3; Meta-Harness +1.4 / +1.8 / +2.2 / −2.1. — [arXiv 2610.25858](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.25858)
- Its findings: "higher plasticity consistently comes at the cost of stability"; global edits to a shared prompt or workspace cause rules from one block to hurt other groups and later blocks to overwrite earlier ones (GEPA condensed seven BIRD SQL sections into one, erasing a +4.0 gain); similarity-based memory retrieval collides when two task groups look alike but need opposite workflows (one group fell 8.0 points). Meta-Harness's apparent stability came partly from a validation gate that rejected many updates, which also limited its plasticity. Conclusion: "current self-evolving agents remain incomplete continual learners". Limits: one base model, three methods, small groups. — [arXiv 2610.25858](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.25858)
- **CL-Bench, "Continual Learning Bench"** (Asawa, ..., Zaharia, Gonzalez; Berkeley and Snorkel AI; arXiv 2606.05661v1, 4 June 2026; abstract only). Six expert-validated domains including software engineering; tasks in a domain share a learnable latent structure (for example codebase layout) that a stateful system can discover and a stateless one cannot; a "gain" metric separates learning from prior capability. Findings: substantial headroom; agents overfit to immediate observations or fail to reuse knowledge; "naive ICL actually outperforms systems built for memory management". No numbers in the abstract. — [arXiv 2606.05661](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05661)
- **AgentStream** (Yan et al.; arXiv 2608.00155, v1 31 July 2026, v2 27 September 2026; abstract only). Turns existing agent benchmarks into configurable task streams under Isolated, Sequential and Interleaved scenarios; five self-evolving methods on three frontier models. Findings: reliability varies across scenarios; benefit is non-monotonic in model strength; no method dominates. — [arXiv 2608.00155](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00155)
- **SEA-Eval** (Jiang et al., Fudan; arXiv 2604.08988v2, 14 April 2026). 32 atomic tasks and 92 sequences: 32 correlated (to measure gain), 30 orthogonal (stability), 30 implicit-intent (alignment), plus a noise condition that preloads 20 unrelated skills. Primary metrics are success rate and token consumption; it argues falling token use across a sequence distinguishes "genuine evolution from pseudo-evolution". On 12 static tasks two frameworks (OpenClaw, GenericAgent, both on Claude Opus 4.6) each reached 100% success while total tokens differed about sevenfold (27,364K versus 3,918K). Two frameworks and one model only. — [arXiv 2604.08988](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.08988v2)
- **ContinualSkillBench** (arXiv 2608.03874) and **AgentCL** with its MemProbe diagnostic (arXiv 2606.02461) were reported by a web search as, respectively, five domain task streams of 100 subtasks each with skill-repository updates after each, and controlled task streams with transfer-gain metrics. Neither paper was opened. — [arXiv 2608.03874](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.03874); [arXiv 2606.02461](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2606.02461)

### Inferences
- The 2026 stream results change what "it works" should mean. A method can raise the end-of-stream score while lowering performance on earlier task groups; a single before-and-after number hides that. The minimum honest report for a self-improvement layer is per-task-group scores over time (so plasticity and forgetting can both be read off), plus cost.
- Interference is caused by global edits to shared context, and the fix the Mila study asks for is isolation of task-specific updates. For a harness this argues for scoped artefacts (path-scoped rules, per-task-family skills, enforcers bound to specific triggers) over edits to one always-loaded prompt, and for an additive, itemised update style over rewrite. This matches ACE's design rationale from round one and the round-one SkillsBench finding on focused skills.
- A validation gate trades plasticity for stability (Meta-Harness in the Mila study). That is the right trade for a production harness, but it means the expected rate of accepted changes is low, and the layer should be judged on avoided regressions as much as on gains.
- CL-Bench's result that naive in-context learning beats dedicated memory systems, SEA-Eval's token-convergence criterion, and AgentStream's non-monotonic benefit all point the same way as round one's "Harness Updating Is Not Harness Benefit": the baseline to beat is "do nothing extra", and token cost must be measured alongside success.
- The surveys' evaluation vocabulary (adaptivity, retention, generalisation, efficiency, safety; FGT, BWT, cost-per-gain) has not yet been adopted by the implementations: none of the repositories in section 3 computes a forgetting or backward-transfer metric. Recording per-task scores per candidate, which they all do, is what makes those metrics computable later.

### Gaps
- No benchmark found measures self-improvement of a coding-agent harness on a stream of tasks within one real repository over weeks; CL-Bench's software-engineering domain is the nearest and its design details were not read.
- CL-Bench, AgentStream, ContinualSkillBench and AgentCL were read at abstract or search-snippet level; method names, scores and the "gain" formula are unverified. The last 14,000 characters of the Mila paper and 13,000 of SEA-Eval were not read.
- Whether any of the 2026 stream benchmarks has an open leaderboard or maintained code was not checked.
- LifelongAgentBench, StuLife, AutoEnv and MemoryAgentBench are reported only as the Gao et al. survey describes them.
- I found no survey or benchmark that quantifies the cost of running a self-improvement loop in deployment (as opposed to an offline search), so cost remains an open problem stated by the surveys without a standard measurement.
